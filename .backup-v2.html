How this works, and the trap it's built around

A mixed equilibrium is not a prediction of what your opponent will do next. It is a pair of mixes with one property: each player's mix makes the other player exactly indifferent between their two actions.

That has a consequence students usually get backwards. If your opponent is playing their equilibrium mix, both of your actions earn exactly the same expected payoff. There is no "better move" to find. So "pick the higher-probability action and you'll win" cannot be right: at equilibrium there is nothing to pick.

The equilibrium is still worth computing. It just pays off in two specific ways, which are the two drills here. Both start by asking for both mixes, because each one answers a different question and you need them both to play.

Read the Play

Your opponent is not at equilibrium, so you get a scouting report on what they actually do. Their equilibrium mix q* is the threshold: if their real mix sits on one side of it, one of your actions is strictly better; on the other side, the other one is. That is what the equilibrium is for in practice.

The Long Game

Same sloppy opponent, but now they adapt, and you are playing 200 rounds rather than one. Exploiting them means going pure, and a pure strategy is readable: the moment they answer it you are pinned at that row's worst cell with nothing left to adjust. Your own mix p* gives up today's edge and buys a payoff no reply can touch. Here "guaranteed" is literally true, and the guarantee is the value of the game.

This one only appears in constant-sum games, where one player's gain really is the other's loss. In a game like The Rooftop Set the two payoff grids are unrelated, your mix guarantees you nothing, and the drill stays on Read the Play.

The bit everyone gets backwards

Your mix is computed from your opponent's payoffs, and theirs from yours. You choose p so that they can't prefer either column. If you find yourself making yourself indifferent while solving for your own mix, you've crossed the wires.
